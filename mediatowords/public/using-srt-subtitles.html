What are SRT subtitles?

SRT subtitles are plain text arranged into numbered cue blocks. Each block contains a start time, an end time and the words to display during that interval. You can open the file in a text editor, but a subtitle editor makes it easier to review timing against video. A separate SRT does not burn text into the picture: your player, editor or publishing workflow must load it as a subtitle track.

The steps below explain the file format and review process; particular import menus and supported styling depend on the application you choose.

To inspect a cue manually, read its number, then the timing line in hours, minutes, seconds and milliseconds, followed by the caption text. Leave a blank line before the next numbered block. Save as plain text with the .srt extension, not a word-processing document. Reopen the saved file to make sure your editor did not append .txt or remove the timing punctuation.

How to use the SRT you export here

Import and preview your export

Create a transcript with the Instagram transcript tool, listen to the recording and correct the editable lines before choosing Download SRT. Save the downloaded file alongside the exact recording you processed. The exported cues include your text corrections, but editing those words does not automatically change their times. Save again after any correction; an older download does not update itself.

In an editor that supports SRT, open the matching video project and use its subtitle or caption import command. Select the new file, enable the track, and preview speech near the beginning, middle and end. Then choose whether your final output should retain a separate subtitle file or render captions into the video. Exporting SRT alone performs neither video rendering nor publishing.

Why TXT and SRT timestamps look different

In the existing paired export, the second line is labelled 00:00:05 in TXT but spans 00:00:05,440 to 00:00:07,360 in SRT. Moving that subtitle to exactly five seconds would introduce a 440-millisecond change. Fewer displayed digits do not by themselves prove a synchronization error.

The same export has one cue ending at 38.720 seconds and the next starting at 39.360 seconds, leaving a 640-millisecond gap. TXT start labels cannot show that gap because they omit end times. Find a distinctive phrase in both files and preview its SRT timing against the audio before shifting anything. These file measurements do not establish exactly when the speaker began talking.

How to check a missing subtitle ending

Listen through the last spoken sentence in the exact source recording, then find its words in the transcript. A gap after the final recognized timestamp may be silence, music or an end card. If the source itself cuts off, obtain a complete recording; a subtitle edit cannot restore absent audio. Check for a closing phrase merged into the preceding line before deciding that content is missing.

If the words appear on the page but not in playback, open the latest downloaded SRT as text and inspect its final blocks. Confirm the filename and download time so you are not reviewing an older export. Correct verified omissions, download again and preview the ending. Added words may require a timing adjustment in your editor because text correction preserves the existing cue boundaries.

How to edit words without breaking timing

Listen before changing names, numbers or technical terms, and identify the occurrence by its surrounding sentence. A word may be wrong once but correct elsewhere, so do not apply an unchecked global replacement. Correct the relevant transcript line or SRT cue, preserve its number and timing line, and keep explanatory notes separate from quoted speech.

Download a fresh copy after editing the on-page transcript, then search that exact file for the corrected phrase. Longer wording may need additional reading time even when its spelling is now accurate. Preview the cue against audio and adjust its timing in your subtitle editor if necessary. Renaming an old file does not incorporate the changes you made on the page.

How to fix garbled letters

Reopen the original download using UTF-8 before overwriting it. An unchanged UTF-8 word such as café can display incorrectly when opened with another encoding; reading the original bytes correctly may restore it. In the retained controlled samples, café survived both UTF-8 and Windows-1252 saves, while İzmir and the emoji survived only the UTF-8 copy when unsupported characters were replaced during saving.

A literal question mark saved in place of a character contains no record of the original letter: in that sample it is byte 3F. Recover a version that still contains the correct characters rather than repeatedly changing the damaged file’s encoding. If the word is already wrong in the on-page transcript, listen and correct the recognition result; encoding changes cannot fix a wrongly recognized word.

How to split a long subtitle line

Insert a single newline at a phrase boundary within one cue, keeping related words together. Both text lines then share the same start and end times; they do not appear in sequence. An empty line separates cue blocks, so avoid inserting a blank separator between lines that should belong to the same subtitle.

The retained constructed example wraps one sentence into lines of 35 and 32 characters using a 36-character target, while keeping its 1.000-to-6.000-second interval and word order. That target is an example, not a universal reading limit. Preview your chosen font, frame size and line placement. To display phrases at different times, give them separate cues checked against the speech.

How to pair subtitles with the correct video

Keep the export version, language and subtitle filename with the source recording. A matching basename helps organization but cannot verify content. In the retained silent fixtures, a subtitle ending at 11 seconds exceeds an 8-second video by three seconds. That mismatch is detectable without speech recognition. Reverse the pairing and a 7-second ending fits a 12-second video even though the file belongs to another fixture.

Therefore, reject an impossible end time but do not treat a passing duration check as proof of a correct match. Two different edits may share a duration. Compare the first and last spoken passages and every edited section with the actual captions. A stored video fingerprint identifies the same bytes later; it does not prove that the words or cue times are correct.

How to sync subtitles after adding an intro

Compare a recognizable spoken phrase near the beginning with another later in the final export. If both have the same delay, measure the added time before the original footage and shift the affected cues by that amount. For a three-second introduction, an original cue from two to four seconds becomes five to seven seconds. Move both boundaries together to preserve duration; this arithmetic applies only when the underlying footage keeps its timing.

Give any new spoken intro its own cues instead of shifting them with the original footage. Keep the original SRT, save the adjusted version separately, and preview the beginning, middle and final sentence against the exported video. Check that the editor uses the newest track rather than a previously imported copy.

Frequently asked questions

Can I edit an SRT file without special software?

To begin for free, use the text editor already available on your device to inspect the file, or use a subtitle-capable editor you already have. Keep an untouched original before making changes.

Why doesn't my subtitle timing match the video?

Cuts, inserted sections and speed changes can produce varying offsets that one global shift cannot repair.

What's the difference between TXT and SRT for captions?

TXT start labels are useful for finding a sentence, while SRT records both boundaries with milliseconds.
